# Facilitator Guide

Read this once, a week before the day. Use the [run sheet](run-sheet.md) on the
day.

## The shape of the day

The morning gives participants a mental model, the rules, and one method. The
afternoon gives them techniques and practice, ending in something they take
home.

```
00 first prompt → 01 how it fails + what not to paste → 02 CRAFT + own task v1
     → lunch →
03 techniques → 04 criteria + verification → 05 own task as a reusable template → 06 Monday plan
```

The participant's own task runs through the whole day: named in 00 intros,
version 1 in 02, re-run in the 03 warm-up, optionally refined in 04, finished in
05, planned for Monday in 06. When you're short of time, protect that thread.

## What first-time government users need from you

- **Permission to be skeptical.** Many arrive worried about getting in trouble,
  being replaced, or looking foolish. Say early that knowing when *not* to use
  AI is a success condition.
- **Concrete rules.** "Use good judgment" is not guidance. Point to the
  traffic light and the agency policy. If the agency's position is unclear, say
  so honestly, and give the conservative default.
- **Mechanics without embarrassment.** Expect people who've never opened the
  tool. Normalize asking for help with the interface; it's not the skill being
  taught.
- **Their work, not tech demos.** Every example is a public-sector task. Resist
  showing off impressive-but-irrelevant capabilities.

## Facilitation moves

- **Run the plain version first.** Every technique lands harder when
  participants have just seen the output it fixes.
- **Ask before you tell.** In live builds, ask the room what to add next before
  you add it.
- **Circulate with one question per activity.** 2A: "Show me your Audience." 03:
  "What did the plain version get wrong?" 04: "What did you change this round,
  just one thing?" 05: "Did your partner have to ask you anything?"
- **Name good catches publicly.** When someone spots a fabrication or a missing
  fact, tell the room. Catching errors is the skill.
- **Results vary; plan for it.** Don't promise that a demo will fail a certain
  way. Promise a comparison. If the plain version comes out fine, ask "would you
  have known if it hadn't?"

## Difficult moments

| Situation | Response |
| --- | --- |
| "AI will take our jobs" | Acknowledge, park it, don't debate. "Today is about you being the person in your office who uses it well and safely." |
| "This is all hype" | Agree that some is. Ask them to judge by the station results, not by your enthusiasm. Skeptics make excellent verifiers; give them that role |
| "My agency says we can't use it at all" | Then today is practice for when it does, and the safety habits apply to any tool. Don't encourage workarounds |
| A legal or policy question you can't answer | "I don't know, and I don't want to guess about your agency's rules. It's on the parking lot, and you'll get an answer in writing." |
| Real sensitive data on someone's screen | Quietly, privately: help them delete the chat and make a stand-in. Never call it out in front of the room |
| A demo produces something offensive or badly wrong | Use it. "This is exactly why you own the output." Show the verify checklist |
| An enthusiast dominates | Make them a table helper with a specific job: "Help your table get signed in" |
| Mixed experience in the room | Every activity has an "if you finish early" line; point fast pairs to it |

## Accessibility

- Every handout is plain Markdown and screen-reader friendly; offer digital
  copies to anyone who prefers them.
- Read every prompt on a slide aloud.
- Allow pairs to choose a driver; don't require everyone to type.
- Keep the room temperature and break times humane; this is a long day.
